PingFederate & Federation

Identity federation lets users from one organization (the Identity Provider) authenticate to applications in another (the Service Provider). PingFederate is the enterprise federation server.

🤝 What is identity federation — and how does Ping let a user from one org access another's app without sharing passwords?

Identity federation lets a user whose account lives in Company A authenticate to Company B's app with no shadow account and no shared password — via a trust relationship between an IdP (knows the user) and an SP (owns the app). The user logs in at the IdP, which issues a signed assertion the SP trusts via the IdP's public key. SAML 2.0 is the enterprise standard: SP-initiated SSO redirects to the IdP with an AuthnRequest, and the IdP POSTs a signed Assertion to the SP's ACS. PingFederate is both — a broker that accepts assertions from many upstream IdPs (AD FS via SAML, Okta/Google via OIDC) and normalizes them into one trusted token, so you can swap an upstream IdP without touching the app. OIDC is the modern JWT-based alternative; Ping supports both.

  • Federation = IdP↔SP trust — no shadow accounts, no shared passwords.
  • SAML 2.0 — signed XML assertions; SP-initiated AuthnRequest → ACS POST.
  • PingFederate brokers many upstream IdPs into one normalized token.
  • Swap an upstream IdP by editing one connection; OIDC is the JWT alternative.

The problem

Every check passed. The signature was real, the assertion was in date, and you are now signed in as somebody else.

IdP-initiated SSO was switched on for the portal tile, and it removed the only thing tying an assertion to the person holding it

Users wanted to click your app's icon on the PingFederate launchpad and land inside, already logged in. That is IdP-initiated SSO: no AuthnRequest from your app, no redirect out — PingFederate just POSTs a signed assertion straight at your ACS URL. One checkbox on the SP connection. Everyone does it.

Now look at what your SP can still verify. Signature: valid, it really came from your IdP. Audience: correct, it names you. NotOnOrAfter: fine. Assertion ID: not seen before. All good — and every one of those checks is about the assertion. Not one of them is about the browser presenting it. In the SP-initiated walk above, the thing that closed that gap was InResponseTo: proof that this response answers a request you sent moments ago to this browser. An unsolicited response has no InResponseTo — the spec requires its absence — so there is nothing to correlate, and an attacker who can make your browser POST an assertion for their account has just signed you into it.

signature valid ✓ audience is you ✓ not expired, not replayed ✓ InResponseTo: absent by design ⚠ whose browser this is: never checked ⚠

Then you upload the quarterly numbers into an account the attacker can log into whenever they like.

A response you never requested is a response you cannot correlate — and correlation is the only thing that binds an assertion to the person in front of you. Signature proves origin. Audience proves destination. Timestamps and the replay cache prove freshness. None of the four says anything about who is holding it. That is the job InResponseTo does, and IdP-initiated SSO is the mode where you agree to do without it.
Bearer means what it says. A SAML assertion's default SubjectConfirmation method is literally …cm:bearer — the same word as Authorization: Bearer, and the same deal: possession is the entire credential. It proves who the assertion is about. It never proves who is carrying it.
You

The assertion is signed by our own IdP. How is a signed assertion a problem?

The assertion

My signature proves I was issued by that IdP and not tampered with afterwards. It says nothing about the route I took to reach you. I am a signed note about a person — anyone who picks me up can hand me in, which is exactly what bearer means, and that is written into my own SubjectConfirmation.

InResponseTo

Which is why I exist. In an SP-initiated login your app generates an AuthnRequest with a fresh ID, keeps it in that browser's session, and I carry that ID back. A response quoting an ID you never issued, or one you already consumed, is not a login — it is somebody else's assertion arriving at your door. In an unsolicited response I am absent, and there is nothing left to check.

Your ACS endpoint

And I am wide open on purpose. CSRF protection is switched off for me in every SAML SP, because the IdP has to POST here without one of your tokens. So a cross-site form POST is not an anomaly on this endpoint — it is the normal case. Whatever distinguishes a real login from a planted one has to live in the message itself.

The mechanism

Six steps. The attacker never touches your server and never forges anything.

  1. The attacker signs in at the IdP as themselves — a real account, a real password, no forgery anywhereeverything so far is legitimate
  2. They capture the signed SAMLResponse from the POST form before it is consumed — a browser dev-tools panel or a SAML tracer extension is enoughthe credential is now portable
  3. They host a page containing a form that auto-submits that SAMLResponse to your ACS URL, and get a victim to open ita link in an email will do
  4. Your SP validates signature, issuer, audience, timestamps and assertion ID. Every check passes, because the assertion is genuine — just not the victim'snothing is wrong with the assertion
  5. No InResponseTo is present, so nothing compares this response to a request. The SP creates a session for the attacker's identity in the victim's browserthe gap, being used
  6. The victim sees your app, logged in, and carries on working — and everything they save lands in an account the attacker can open at willexfiltration by consent

Step 6 is what makes this worth taking seriously, because the usual instinct is "so what, they logged someone in." Ordinary CSRF makes the victim act as themselves under attacker instruction. Login CSRF inverts it: the victim acts as the attacker, voluntarily, inside an account the attacker owns — uploading a document, saving a note, linking a payment method, connecting a data source. Nothing is stolen in transit. It is handed over.

Three arrivals, one door, and the door cannot tell them apart on anything except that field. Which reframes the whole decision: enabling IdP-initiated SSO is not "adding a convenient second way in" — it is telling your ACS endpoint to stop distinguishing between rows one and three. That is why the fix is a connection setting rather than code, and why the best version of the portal tile is one that deep-links into your app and lets it start an ordinary SP-initiated login.

There is nothing to compare it against: the victim may have no session with you at all, and that is the ordinary case for this attack. SAML has no notion of "this assertion belongs to that browser" — the default SubjectConfirmation method is bearer, the spec's own word for "whoever holds it". The Subject names who the assertion is about; it has never named who is presenting it.

Correct — and notice the attacker forged nothing and never touched your server. Every property you rely on held: the IdP really issued it, it really names your audience, it really is in date. The missing property is the one no cryptography supplies, which is that this response answers a request this browser made. Restore that by preferring SP-initiated SSO and requiring InResponseTo.

This is what you want, and it is what you get once IdP-initiated is off — but it is not the behaviour of an SP configured to accept it. A response carrying no InResponseTo is, by the specification, a perfectly legitimate unsolicited login; an SP that supports the flow has to accept it, because rejecting it would break the very portal tile the flow was enabled for. You cannot have the tile and the check at the same time, which is the actual decision on the table.

Where you have seen this before

Every federation protocol eventually invents the same field, under a different name.

OIDC's state and nonce are this exact idea, redesigned

The relying party generates both, stores them against the browser's session, and refuses any response that does not echo them back. That is InResponseTo with the lesson already learned — which is also why OIDC has no equivalent of IdP-initiated login as a normal mode. The successor protocol made the correlation mandatory instead of optional.

same field, better defaults

Login CSRF — the direction nobody threat-models

Everyone defends the case where an attacker makes you act as yourself. Almost nobody models the inverse, because "logging a user in" does not sound like an attack until you notice whose account they were logged into. Worth a slot in your threat model in its own right: any endpoint that can establish a session from a cross-site POST is a login-CSRF surface.

the attack that gives you something

Bearer tokens, cookies, API keys — one property, three names

A signed JWT you paste into a header behaves identically: valid signature, correct audience, in date, and utterly indifferent to who is sending it. It is why session fixation, token theft and this attack all rhyme, and why the mitigations always come down to the same two moves — shorten the window, or bind the credential to something the attacker cannot move.

possession is the whole credential
The whole page on a napkin: the IdP asserts who you are, the SP trusts it instead of keeping passwords, PingFederate holds the SP Connection that names the entity ID, ACS URL, signing certificate and attribute contract, and SP-initiated SAML runs AuthnRequest → authenticate → signed assertion POSTed to the ACS → session. The sentence worth carrying: the assertion is a bearer credential, so InResponseTo is what ties it to this browser — which is precisely what IdP-initiated SSO gives up.

1 — Federation Core Concepts

Identity Provider (IdP)

The authority on who the user is
The system that authenticates the user and asserts their identity. Your company's PingFederate server is the IdP.

The IdP holds the user directory (or connects to one via LDAP/AD). When authentication succeeds, it issues a token or assertion proving who the user is.

Service Provider (SP)

The application consuming the assertion
The application the user wants to access. The SP trusts the IdP to vouch for users rather than maintaining its own username/password store.

In practice: your Spring Boot API or a SaaS app like Salesforce, ServiceNow, or Workday can all be SPs trusting the same corporate IdP.

SAML 2.0

XML-based enterprise SSO standard
Security Assertion Markup Language. The dominant enterprise SSO protocol for B2B federation. PingFederate excels at SAML.

A SAML assertion is a signed XML document containing: who the user is, when they authenticated, and their attributes (email, roles, department).

Used for: corporate SSO to SaaS apps, inter-company federation ("log in with your company account").

OIDC / OAuth2

Modern web & API auth standard
OpenID Connect (OIDC) sits on top of OAuth2. PingFederate also acts as an OAuth2 Authorization Server / OIDC Provider for modern apps.

Produces JWT tokens instead of XML assertions. Used for: Angular SPAs, mobile apps, API-to-API auth (Client Credentials).

Connection (SP Connection)

PingFederate's trust configuration
In PingFederate admin, an SP Connection defines the trust relationship between PingFederate (as IdP) and a specific Service Provider.

You configure: the SP's entity ID, ACS URL (where assertions are sent), signing certificates, attribute contract (which user attributes to include), and authentication policy.

Attribute Contract

What user data is sent in the assertion
Defines which user attributes PingFederate includes in the SAML assertion or OIDC token sent to the SP.

Common attributes: email, displayName, memberOf (AD groups), employeeId, department.

The SP uses these to set up the user's session and apply authorization rules.
