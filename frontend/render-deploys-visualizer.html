Render — from git push to live URL

or, the two ways a perfectly good app comes up dead on a platform that promised to do everything for you

🚀 What is Render, and what is it actually doing between your git push and a working URL?

Render is a PaaS — a Platform as a Service. You hand it a repo; it hands you back an HTTPS URL. In between it clones your code, runs your build, starts your app in a container, and waits for that app to prove it is alive before sending anyone to it. Compare that to the AWS/GCP way, where you'd assemble a VM or a cluster, a load balancer, a TLS certificate and a deploy pipeline yourself: the pieces still exist here — you just don't own them. That is the trade, and it's a good one right up until something fails, at which point you need to know which piece failed and you can't SSH in to look.

  • A "service" is your app, running. A web service takes HTTP traffic; a static site is just files on a CDN.
  • Render picks the port, not you. It sets $PORT and you must bind it — on 0.0.0.0, not localhost.
  • The health check is the gate. Until your app answers it, the new version gets zero traffic and the old one keeps serving.
  • Free instances sleep after 15 idle minutes. The next visitor waits about a minute for the container to come back.

Why "it works locally" proves nothing

Locally you choose the port and localhost is reachable because you're already inside the machine. In a container, "localhost" means inside that container — a listener bound there is invisible to everything outside it, which is exactly the failure that produces "no open ports detected".

A deploy that fails is not an outage

This is the part worth internalising early: Render only switches traffic to the new version after it passes the health check. A broken push leaves the previous version serving. The bad news is the same rule cancels a deploy when the health check is merely unreachable — a protected endpoint, for instance.

Spring / CIAM: two lines of application.properties decide whether your Spring Boot app survives contact with a platform: server.address=0.0.0.0 and server.port=${PORT:8080}. The ${PORT:8080} form reads the platform's variable in production and falls back to 8080 on your laptop, so one file works in both places.
>_

Your build went green. The dashboard says Live. You open the URL and get a 502 — or worse, a page that hangs for fifty seconds and then works perfectly. Neither is random. Each one is Render telling you something specific about your app, and by the end of this page you'll read both at a glance.

The idea

A platform is a trade: you give up choices and get back everything you'd otherwise have to assemble.

Put a Spring Boot app on the internet with AWS and "deploy" expands into a shopping list: a registry for the image, a service to run it, a load balancer in front, a certificate, DNS, a log destination, and an IAM role tying them together. Every one of those is a thing you can get wrong. Render collapses that list into two commands and a repo URL. It builds your code, runs it in a container, puts HTTPS in front of it, watches a health check, rolls each new version out without dropping a request, and — the part people don't expect — refuses to send traffic to a version that won't come up.

The price is that you get Render's opinions instead of your own. That is a genuinely good deal right up until the day it isn't, and knowing exactly where the line sits is most of what this section is for.

You

git push origin main

Render

Saw commit 4f1c2ab. Building… image made. Ran your pre-deploy. Started the process — something is listening on 10000. Health check on /actuator/health came back 200. Traffic is yours. I'll SIGTERM the old instance in sixty seconds.

Bind 0.0.0.0:$PORT — or you don't exist.

Say it out loud now and save yourself an evening. Render hands every web service a PORT environment variable — the default value is 10000 — and forwards inbound requests to whatever is listening there. There are exactly two ways to fail this, and almost every first-time Render deploy in every language fails one of them:

  • Hardcoding a different port. Your app listens on 8080 because it always has. Render looks for a listener, can usually find an alternate port, and when it can't, the deploy itself fails with a port-detection error in the logs — the service never goes live at all.
  • Binding to localhost. This is the sneaky one, because it works perfectly on your laptop. 127.0.0.1 means "this machine's own loopback, and nothing outside it". Render's proxy lives outside the container, knocks on the door, and nobody answers. Same symptom, completely different cause.
  1. you push a commit — Render sees it and starts a deploytrigger the old version is still serving every request, and will be for the whole of this list
  2. your build command runs on a build machine that is not your laptopbuild which is why a dependency you only have installed locally fails here, and nowhere else
  3. the artifact is packaged and a fresh container is startedstart your start command runs; nothing is listening yet as far as Render knows
  4. Render scans for a process listening on $PORTthe gate bound to localhost instead of 0.0.0.0? Invisible. "No open ports detected."
  5. it requests your health check path and waits for a 2xx or 3xxprove it a 401 is a 4xx, so a health endpoint behind auth reads as unhealthy — and cancels the deploy
  6. only now does traffic switch to the new versionthe payoff the old container gets SIGTERM 60 seconds later, so in-flight requests can finish

Read steps 4 and 5 together, because they are the two places a perfectly working application fails here. Both are the same kind of mistake: your app is doing exactly what you told it to, and what you told it to do is invisible to the platform. Nothing in your logs will look wrong — the app started fine, because from the app's point of view it did.

Motivation

Why spend a page on a platform whose whole pitch is that it needs no explaining?

01
It's the shortest path from repo to HTTPS

Nothing else in this track gets a real certificate and a real URL in front of your code with as little ceremony. That makes it the right place to host the projects you build here.

02

build → pre-deploy → start. Once you know which of the three a problem lives in, you've already halved the debugging.

03
The shape transfers

Cloud Run and App Runner work the same way — read a port from the environment, answer a health probe, get traffic. Learn it here where there's nothing else in the way.

04
The failures are few and famous

The port, the health check, the fifteen-minute nap, and the database with a thirty-day fuse. Four traps. This page defuses all four.

Anatomy of a Render web service

Five settings. Click each one — the last two are where the interesting failures live.

the repo and branch — this is the pipeline

Connecting a repo and naming a branch is the whole trigger. There is no workflow file to write for the deploy half: push to that branch and Render rebuilds. You can change when that happens — autoDeployTrigger: commit (the default, deploy on every push), checksPass (wait for your CI to go green first, which is how you keep a failing test suite out of production), or off for deploy-on-demand only. A commit message containing [skip render] is skipped entirely, which is the polite way to push a README fix.

the runtime — and the one that isn't there

Render has native runtimes for node, python, ruby, go, rust and elixir: point at the repo and it knows how to build it. There is no native Java runtime. JVM apps — Spring Boot, Kotlin, Scala — go through runtime: docker and a Dockerfile you write yourself. That sounds like a downgrade and mostly isn't: you get the exact JDK you asked for, and the image is reproducible. (Two other special values: image pulls a prebuilt container from a registry, and static is a CDN-hosted static site.)

the build command — one shot, then it's gone

Compilation and dependency install: npm ci && npm run build, ./mvnw -DskipTests package. It runs on a build machine, not on the instance that will serve traffic, with a 120-minute ceiling. The consequence people trip on: anything you install or generate here that doesn't end up in the image or the repo is not there at run time. Build writes; start reads.

the start command — the one rule lives here

java -jar target/app.jar, node server.js, gunicorn app:app. Render runs this and then watches for something to accept a connection. You get 15 minutes to be listening. This is where 0.0.0.0:$PORT is either honoured or quietly ignored — and on a Docker service, the Docker Command (if you set one) overrides your Dockerfile's CMD, so check both before blaming the app.

the health check — the gate on every deploy

A path starting with / that answers any 2xx or 3xx when the app is ready, and any 4xx or 5xx when it isn't. It must answer within five seconds. Nothing else on this page changes behaviour as much: until this returns healthy, the new instance gets zero traffic, and if it never does, Render gives up after 15 minutes, cancels the deploy, and keeps the old version serving. Leave it unset and you lose that safety net — a broken build can go live simply because it managed to open a socket.

Your Spring Boot app runs fine locally on 8080. On Render the deploy fails with a port error and the service never starts. What's the actual fix?

A memory problem shows up as an OOM kill in the logs, not a port error — and the message you got named the port specifically. Paying for a bigger instance to fix a one-line config bug is a bad afternoon.

Right, and note that it's two fixes in one line of config. ${PORT:8080} uses Render's port when it's there and 8080 on your laptop when it isn't, so you don't need a separate profile. 0.0.0.0 makes the app answer on every interface rather than only its own loopback.

EXPOSE is documentation. It records which port the image intends to use and publishes absolutely nothing — the container behaves identically with it, without it, or with the wrong number in it. This is one of the most widely believed false things about Docker.

The reference

The settings, the variables and the buttons that cover a working month.

The three commands, in the order they run
buildCommandCompile and install. On a build machine, 120-minute ceiling. Writes the artifact.
preDeployCommandOptional. After the build, before any traffic, on its own one-off instance — 30-minute ceiling. This is the migration slot.
startCommandRuns your process on the real instance. 15 minutes to be listening on 0.0.0.0:$PORT.
dockerCommandDocker services only — overrides the image's CMD. Check it before you blame the Dockerfile.
Variables Render sets for you, on every service
PORTThe port to bind. Defaults to 10000. The single most important variable on this page.
RENDERAlways the string "true" — how code detects it's running on Render at all.
RENDER_EXTERNAL_URLYour full onrender.com URL. Handy for absolute links and OAuth redirect URIs.
RENDER_GIT_COMMITThe deployed commit SHA — put it in /actuator/info and never guess what's live again.
IS_PULL_REQUEST"true" inside a preview environment. Every value is a string, including this one.
RENDER_SERVICE_NAMEWhich service you are. Useful in logs when three services share a log drain.
When a deploy goes wrong
RollbackRe-deploy a previous successful deploy. The fastest correct move at 2am — fix forward in the morning.
Clear build cache & deployCached layers are usually a gift and occasionally a lie. Reach for this when a build succeeds locally and not here.
[skip render]In a commit message: don't deploy this one. For README and comment-only commits.

Spring Security is protecting everything, so /actuator/health answers 401 to Render's health check. You push a perfectly good commit.

Predict before you pick: what do your users experience?

This is the intuition to unlearn. A failing health check doesn't produce a degraded live version — it produces no live version. Traffic is never handed over, so the 401 is invisible to users and visible only to you, in the deploy log.

Exactly. 401 is a 4xx, 4xx means unhealthy, and an unhealthy new instance never receives traffic. After 15 minutes Render cancels the deploy and keeps routing to the instances already running. Your users see the old version, working. The bug is real and costs you a deploy, not an outage — which is precisely the trade a health check exists to make.

Restart-looping is what happens to a service that crashes. This one starts fine and answers every request correctly; it just answers the health check with a 401. Render's response to unhealthy-on-deploy is to withhold traffic, not to restart.

Put it together

Take an app that only runs locally and make it deployable — predict each output before you reveal it.

Make the port a variable, not a constant

One line covers both environments: Render's PORT when it exists, 8080 on your laptop when it doesn't. The address line is the half everyone forgets, and it's the half that makes the container reachable from outside itself.

server:
  address: 0.0.0.0
  port: ${PORT:8080}
$ PORT=10000 java -jar target/app.jar
Tomcat started on port 10000 (http) with context path '/'
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' http://localhost:10000/actuator/health
200
two lines, and they fail differently. Drop address and the app runs perfectly while Render reports "no open ports" — it is listening, just not anywhere reachable. Drop the ${PORT:8080} fallback and it is production that works and your laptop that breaks.
Give Render something to ask "are you alive?"

The endpoint has to be reachable without credentials — Render's prober has no token and no cookie. If your filter chain protects everything by default, this one line is the difference between a deploy that ships and a deploy that silently cancels.

http.authorizeHttpRequests(a -> a
    .requestMatchers("/actuator/health").permitAll()
    .anyRequest().authenticated());
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' https://devhub-api.onrender.com/actuator/health
200

# and the rest of the API is still protected:
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' https://devhub-api.onrender.com/api/users
401
the permitAll line is the whole step. Everything above it just makes the endpoint exist; that one line is what lets an unauthenticated prober reach it, and without it the endpoint returns 401 — a 4xx, which reads as unhealthy.
Push, and watch the phases go by

The log is the state machine. Each of the three commands announces itself, and the phase a failure appears in tells you where to look — a stack trace during build is a compile problem, the same trace during start is a config problem.

$ git commit -m "bind PORT, open the health endpoint" && git push
==> Cloning from https://github.com/you/devhub-api (commit 4f1c2ab)
==> Running build command './mvnw -B -DskipTests package'...
    BUILD SUCCESS
==> Running pre-deploy command 'psql "$DATABASE_URL" -f schema.sql'...
    CREATE TABLE
==> Running 'java -jar target/app.jar'
    Tomcat started on port 10000
==> Your service is live 🎉
the interesting gap is between Build successful and Live. Almost every real failure happens in there, which is why "the build passed" tells you much less than it feels like it should.
Prove the zero-downtime claim instead of believing it

Hammer the service with one request a second, then trigger a deploy from the dashboard. If the rollout works the way the docs describe, you will not see a single failure — the old instance keeps answering right up until the new one is healthy.

$ while true; do
    curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code} ' https://devhub-api.onrender.com/actuator/health
    sleep 1
  done
200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200
#        ^ deploy started here          ^ traffic switched here
# zero non-200s: the old instance served every request until the
# new one passed its health check, then got SIGTERM 60 seconds later.
the result is the line that is not there: no non-200 in the whole run. That is the health check doing its job — traffic only ever moved to a container that had already answered.
The CIAM version of this page: the single most common way a Spring Security app fails to deploy on any platform is that the health endpoint is behind the filter chain. Render's prober arrives with no Authorization header, your chain does exactly what you told it to, and a 401 comes back — which the platform reads as "this instance is not ready". Nothing is broken; two correct systems disagreed. The one to remember: a health check is an unauthenticated endpoint by design. Keep it boring — liveness only, no user data, no database round-trip you can't afford under five seconds.
There are no dumb questions

Isn't this just Heroku with a different logo?

The lineage is real and the shape is deliberately familiar — but three things differ enough to matter. Render's Blueprint (render.yaml) describes your whole environment as a file in your repo, so an environment can be recreated rather than remembered; that's the next page. Preview environments clone the entire stack per pull request, database included. And services talk over a private network, so a database or an internal API can be genuinely unreachable from the public internet rather than merely undocumented. The free tier also behaves differently: it sleeps, where Heroku's simply stopped existing.

If it builds a container anyway, why not just run Docker or Kubernetes myself?

Because you'd be rebuilding the parts of this page you didn't have to think about. Render's rollout — start the new instance, health-check it, switch traffic, SIGTERM the old one after 60 seconds, purge the edge cache — is roughly a Kubernetes Deployment with a readiness probe and a rolling update strategy, and you get it without writing or maintaining any of it. The honest answer to "when should I graduate?" is: when you need something Render doesn't have an opinion about. Multi-region traffic policies, a service mesh, GPU scheduling, a custom autoscaler. Until then, the platform is doing real work and the complexity you'd be adding buys nothing. Pick the smallest thing that will hold your app — and know why you'd move.

What actually happens while my free service is "asleep"?

The container is stopped, not paused. Fifteen minutes without inbound traffic and it spins down; the next request has to wait roughly a minute for it to start again, and Render shows a loading page in the meantime. Three consequences worth internalising. In-memory state is gone — sessions, caches, that Map you were using as a database. Scheduled work doesn't run: a @Scheduled job inside a sleeping web service simply doesn't happen, which is what a cron service is for. And first impressions suffer — a recruiter clicking your portfolio link at 9pm waits a minute on a blank-ish screen and concludes the app is broken. The fix is a paid instance, not a clever keep-alive pinger; the pinger keeps the service awake and does nothing at all about the free database's 30-day clock.
